When reviewing contractor websites across roofing, plumbing, HVAC, and other home services trades, a pattern shows up quickly: most sites fail the same way. Not because they look bad, but because they make visitors work too hard to answer the most basic questions. This guide covers the contractor website must haves that actually move people from "browsing" to "calling."

This is not about design trends. It is about the information hierarchy that home service buyers follow when they land on a site and decide whether to stay or bounce.

## Contractor Website Must-Haves Start With the Phone Number

Put your phone number in the top-right corner of every page. Make it large enough to read on a phone without zooming. If someone is on a mobile device and your number is buried at the bottom of the homepage, you have already lost the lead.

On desktop, the number should be in the header. On mobile, it should be in a sticky bar or pinned at the top.

Beyond placement, the number needs to actually work. This sounds obvious, but when reviewing contractor websites it is common to find numbers that go to a voicemail that has not been set up, or that ring through to a personal phone with no business greeting. If someone calls at 7pm and gets a confusing voicemail, they move to the next result.

Related to this: if you do not answer after hours, you need a text-back system. Someone searching for a plumber at 10pm is not waiting until tomorrow to hear back. More on this below.

## What Services You Offer and Where You Work

Visitors need to know within 10 seconds whether you can help them. That means two things need to be obvious from your homepage:

**What you do:** List your main services clearly. Not in a design element that requires hovering, not buried in a PDF. A simple list or card layout on the homepage works. If you do roofing, list roofing, roof repair, roof replacement, gutters, and storm damage. If you do HVAC, list installation, repair, maintenance, and emergency service. Specificity builds trust.

**Where you work:** Name the cities and counties you serve. Homeowners are territorial - they want to know you serve their area before they spend time reading about you. If you show up in a search for "HVAC repair Austin" and your site never mentions Austin, some visitors will bounce immediately.

This is also an SEO issue. Pages that name service areas rank for local searches. A homepage that says "serving homeowners in the DFW metroplex" is doing more work than one that says "serving homeowners everywhere."

If you serve multiple trade types (like a full-service home improvement company), a clear services page with individual pages per service is worth building. This is not just for SEO - it also lets you send someone directly to your roofing page when you are running a roofing campaign.

## Contractor Website Must-Haves: Trust Signals That Actually Work

Buyers do not take your word for it. They look for external signals that confirm you are real, licensed, and competent. The ones that actually matter:

**Google reviews displayed on-site.** A widget that pulls your live Google rating is more powerful than a page of quotes you wrote yourself. The number matters too - a company with 4.7 stars and 180 reviews reads as more trustworthy than one with 5 stars and 8 reviews.

**Before and after photos.** This is the most underused asset in contractor marketing. Real job photos - not stock images - tell visitors what your work actually looks like. A roofing company with 20 photo sets of replaced roofs is showing proof in the most direct way possible. Label the photos with the city and type of work (e.g., "Roof replacement in Plano - 2025") for double duty as SEO content.

**Licensing and insurance information.** If your state requires a contractor license, show your license number. "Licensed, bonded, and insured" in the footer is table stakes. If you can list the specific license number, do it. Some buyers will look it up to verify - and that is a good sign.

**Years in business.** If you have been operating for 8 or more years, say so clearly. Longevity signals stability. A company that has been working in the same market for a decade is less likely to disappear mid-project.

**Owner or team photos.** A headshot or crew photo - even a basic one - makes the business feel real and approachable. Buyers hire people, not logos. A photo taken on a job site or in front of a branded truck works better than a generic office headshot - it reinforces what you actually do.

## A Clear, Specific Call to Action on Every Page

One of the most common issues found when reviewing contractor websites is the vague CTA. "Contact us" on a button that leads to a contact form is less effective than "Get a Free Estimate" or "Book a Service Call."

The CTA should match what the buyer is ready to do. On a service page, that is usually requesting a quote. On a blog post, it might be scheduling a consultation. On a homepage, it could be a quick form with name, phone, and service type.

Forms should be short - three to five fields is the ceiling. Every field you add reduces submission rate. Name, phone number, service needed, and maybe zip code is enough to qualify a lead and call them back. If you need more information, get it on the phone.

## Response Time and Availability Information

This is not just an operations problem - it is a marketing problem. When a buyer sees "we typically respond within 24 hours" on a contact form, that is not reassuring for someone whose water heater just failed.

If you offer emergency service, say so prominently. If you have extended hours, post them. If you have a weekend crew, feature that. Availability is a competitive differentiator in home services, and most contractor sites do not communicate it well.

For businesses that cannot always answer the phone, a missed-call text-back system changes the equation. When someone calls and does not get an answer, an automatic text goes out within a minute: "Hey, this is [Name] from [Company]. I missed your call - what can I help you with?" This keeps the lead in the conversation even when no one is available to pick up.

Sites that mention response protocols - even briefly - tend to generate better-quality leads because buyers who reach out already have a reasonable expectation of how the process works.

## Auditing Your Site and Using This Checklist

### How to Audit Your Contractor Website

Use this step-by-step process to evaluate whether your site has the critical must-haves:

1. **Check visibility and contact options:** On mobile, verify your phone number is visible at the top without scrolling. Test that it's clickable. Confirm your service area is named within the first two paragraphs of your homepage.

2. **Review services and proof:** List all services shown on your homepage and services pages. Count your before-and-after photos. If you have fewer than 10 real job photos, that's a gap. Check whether Google reviews are displayed anywhere on the site.

3. **Verify trust signals:** Look for your license number, founding year, and team photos. These should be findable within 30 seconds. If they're missing, they're costing you leads.

4. **Test the conversion path:** Fill out your contact form as if you were a real buyer. Count the fields - if there are more than 5, reduce them. Check that the CTA button copy is specific, not generic.

5. **Confirm technical quality:** Load your site on mobile and desktop. Check that pages load in under 3 seconds, no images are broken, and the URL shows https. Test at least two service pages to ensure the layout works on both devices.

After completing these five steps, compare your findings to the full checklist below.

### The Practical Contractor Website Must-Haves Checklist

Here is a consolidated checklist to run against your own site or evaluate a competitor:

**Contact and visibility:**
- Phone number visible in header on both desktop and mobile
- Phone number is clickable on mobile
- Business email or contact form on the contact page
- Hours of operation listed
- Service area named explicitly (cities, counties, or regions)

**Services and proof:**
- Services listed clearly on homepage or navigation
- Individual pages per major service type
- Before and after photos for real jobs
- Google reviews widget or recent reviews displayed

**Trust and credibility:**
- License and insurance status stated
- Years in business or founding year mentioned
- Owner or team photo included
- Business address (even a general city) shown

**Conversion elements:**
- Primary CTA visible above the fold
- Contact form limited to 5 fields or fewer
- CTA copy is specific (not just "contact us")
- Response time or availability information present

**Technical basics:**
- Site loads in under 3 seconds on mobile
- No broken links or missing images
- SSL certificate active (https in URL)
- Mobile layout works without horizontal scrolling

A quick way to test load time: open your homepage in a private/incognito browser window on a phone with your Wi-Fi off and count the seconds until the above-the-fold content appears. If it takes more than three seconds, image compression and hosting performance are the most common fixes. PageSpeed Insights (free, from Google) gives you a more detailed breakdown with specific recommendations.

Running through this list on your site takes about 20 minutes. The gaps it reveals are usually fixable without a complete redesign.

## Common Mistakes and Questions

### What Most Contractor Sites Get Wrong

The common failure modes when reviewing home service websites:

**Too much about the company, not enough about the customer.** The first paragraph of many contractor homepages starts with "We are a family-owned business founded in..." That is fine context, but buyers want to know what you can do for them. Lead with the outcome, not the origin story.

**No mobile optimization.** More than 60 percent of local service searches happen on mobile devices. If your site's contact form does not render correctly on an iPhone, you are losing leads from your most active channel.

**Outdated photos or no photos.** Stock contractor photos are identifiable and do not build trust. Real job photos - even basic ones taken with a phone - perform better. A blurry real photo beats a polished stock image for credibility.

**No connection between reviews and website.** Many contractors have strong Google review profiles but never display them on their site. The two platforms should reinforce each other. If someone finds your site first, seeing your Google rating on the page can be the detail that convinces them to call.

**Generic service descriptions.** "We provide quality roofing services" tells a buyer nothing. "We handle asphalt shingle replacement, flat roof repair, and metal roofing installation across [County]" tells them exactly what you do and whether you can help.

If you have spotted issues in your own setup, the next step is usually prioritizing which ones to fix first. Some gaps cost more leads than others. ### Frequently Asked Questions About Contractor Websites

**How often should I update my before-and-after photos?**
Add new real job photos at least monthly. This keeps your portfolio feeling current and gives you fresh content for SEO. If visitors see photos dated three years ago, they wonder if you're still active.

**Should I hide my pricing on my website?**
Not unless you're doing high-end custom work where every job is unique. For most contractor services, listing price ranges or a clear "call for quote" approach builds more trust than secrecy. If competitors are showing pricing, you lose a conversion point by staying silent.

**What if I only have a few Google reviews?**
Start displaying them anyway. Five honest reviews outperform no reviews. Focus on getting more reviews from recent clients - ask every finished job for a review, and you'll build momentum. See [how contractors build a Google review profile systematically](https://fieldcrewai.com/blog/contractor-google-reviews/) for a practical starting point.

**Do I really need a contact form if I have a phone number?**
Yes. Not every visitor is ready to call. A form option captures people who want to describe their job first or send a message at 11pm when they're researching. Offer both - the choice increases conversions.

**How do I know if my website is actually generating leads?**
Track phone calls with a dedicated number on your site, and use form analytics to see submission counts. If you're not measuring it, you don't know what's working. Add UTM codes to paid ads so you can trace which traffic sources produce calls.
